Path 02 · The compression trick

Super­position

Path 01 ended on a cliffhanger: a model has room for thousands of clean, independent concept-directions, but needs millions of concepts. Here's the trick it invents — and why that trick is the single biggest reason neural networks are hard to read.

01The squeeze

The arithmetic that doesn't work

Count what a language model must represent: every word sense in every language, every person, place, API, idiom, chemical, plot trope. Now count the fully-independent directions its space can hold — at most one per dimension. The gap is brutal:

MILLIONS+
concepts worth knowing
VS
~THOUSANDS
independent directions available

Option one: keep only a few thousand concepts, stay clean. Option two: cheat. Real models cheat.

02The toy experiment

Watch a model choose to cheat

In 2022, Anthropic built the smallest possible version of the dilemma: a tiny network asked to store 5 features in a 2-dimensional space, then trained it while varying one knob — sparsity, how rarely each feature is active. The solutions it found are the whole story of this path.

Drag the knob and watch the model's strategy change:

5 features · 2 dimensions · what the trained model keeps

In the paper this isn't gradual — it's a sharp phase change, like water freezing. And the crammed features snap into clean geometry: opposite pairs, then a perfect pentagon. The compression has crystal structure.

03Why the cheat works

Almost everything is off, almost always

Overlapping directions should be a disaster — activating one concept smears onto its neighbors. The rescue: most concepts are inactive most of the time. No sentence is about DNA, Korean grammar, and mortgage rates at once. If two overlapping features rarely fire together, collisions are rare — and the network's nonlinearity squashes the leftover noise.

Each square is a feature. Watch how often two overlapping ones collide:

Feature activity over time · red = two overlapping features colliding
collisions: 0

Sparsity is the license for superposition. Remember this — the fix in Path 03 exploits exactly the same property.

04The symptom you can see

Polysemantic neurons: the messy drawer

Here's why any of this matters for reading models. A neuron is just one axis of the space — and superposition guarantees that concept-directions don't line up with the axes. So when researchers stare at one neuron of a real model, they see it fire for a grab-bag of unrelated things. Anthropic's 2023 paper describes a neuron responding to academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests, and Korean text:

One neuron · four unrelated triggers · this is normal, not rare
firing ▸…as shown in (Smith et al., 2019)…
firing ▸"Wait," she said, "you can't be serious."
firing ▸GET /api/v2/users HTTP/1.1
firing ▸그는 조용히 문을 열었다

The neuron isn't confused — it's shared. Superposition means the model's real concepts are rotated off the axes we can easily look at. So: can we rotate them back?
