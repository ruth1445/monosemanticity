Path 01 · The basics, geometrically

Visualizing
Latent Space

A language model's entire mental state — everything it "has in mind" while reading — is one long list of numbers. Which means it is one location in a vast geometric space. Everything else in interpretability builds on this one idea. Take it slow.

01Every thought is a point

The space where thinking happens

When a model reads text, each layer of the network summarizes what it has understood so far as a list of numbers — the activation vector. A list of numbers is a coordinate: three numbers pick a point in 3D space, and the model's 4,096 numbers pick a point in a 4,096-dimensional one. That space is called latent space, and one "thought" = one point in it.

We fed a model thousands of tiny snippets and recorded where each one landed. Drag to look around; hover a dot to see the text that put the model there.

Drag to rotate · Hover a point

Nothing about this arrangement was designed. The model chose where every point goes — and already, just from that choice, structure is visible.

Honesty checkpoint

You're looking at a shadow

The real space has thousands of dimensions; your screen has two. Everything on this page is a projection — the same way this 3D cube casts a flat 2D shadow. Shadows lose detail but keep the structure that matters: what's near, what's far, what lines up. Keep that caveat in your pocket for every visualization you ever see of a neural network.

02Distance is meaning

Similar thoughts land near each other

The clusters weren't a coincidence. The model is trained to predict text, and predicting text about cats requires the same "mental machinery" as predicting text about dogs — so the model learns to place those states nearby. Distance in latent space = difference in meaning. This is the property that makes everything measurable.

Test it. Pick a sentence the model has never seen, and watch where its point lands — then check its nearest neighbors. Watch what happens with the two "bank" sentences: same word, two completely different neighborhoods.

The model was never told what a "bank" is. It just noticed that riverbank sentences behave like nature sentences, and money-bank sentences behave like finance ones — so it stores them apart. Context decides the point, not the word.

03Meaning has direction

Concepts aren't places — they're arrows

Here's the jump that unlocks everything. If distance carries meaning, then so does direction: the step from "man" to "king" points the same way as the step from "woman" to "queen." That shared arrow is the concept of royalty, as far as the model is concerned. Concepts live in latent space as directions you can move in, not points you visit.

04Human concepts vs the model's

To you, "cat" is a category. To the model, it's a dial.

Humans treat concepts as boxes: a thing is a cat or it isn't. The model treats every concept as an axis with a volume knob — a thought sits at "0.8 cat, 0.1 French, 0.9 formal" all at once. No box, just coordinates. Every point you saw in Scene 01 is really a recipe: a little of many directions added together.

Two honest complications. Real thoughts blend thousands of dials, not three. And many of the model's dials correspond to no human word at all — figuring out which directions mean what is the entire project of interpretability.

05The catch

There are more concepts than directions

A space with 4,096 dimensions has room for exactly 4,096 directions that are fully independent — mutually perpendicular, zero crosstalk. But a model needs to represent millions of concepts: every animal, every name, every idiom in every language, every API. The arithmetic does not work.

Feel the squeeze in the smallest possible space. This plane has room for 2 clean directions. Keep adding concepts:

The model's answer to this squeeze is a brilliant, messy compression trick called superposition — and it's the reason neural networks are so hard to read. That's Path 02.
